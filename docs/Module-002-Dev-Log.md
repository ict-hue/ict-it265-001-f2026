#### This dev log will be a bit different, as it mainly entails my thoughts regarding energy systems. 
Most of my time, unfortunately, was focused on the IGDA game jam, and I was not particularly focused on the game for this class. That being said:

I have come to a conclusion regarding why opportunity cost is difficult for newer players in games. 

As stated prior, I have been trying to experiment with energy systems in turn based games. However, this delved into a more interesting topic; opportunity cost. As a longtime Pokémon player, opportunity cost is a fundamental aspect of how one plays the game. Swapping Pokémon typically has a negative impact on tempo, and mechanics are in play that opt to harm or strengthen this action (eg. stealth rocks vs. pivot moves). However, tempo also has a strong impact in card games. As someone who has played MTG for years, I also have a decent sense of tempo. However, I cannot justifiably use myself as a metric for research. 

So I gifted a friend Slay the Spire II. They were **bad**.

They would almost always end combats with a net loss of health, but played very defensively on their turns. So what happened?

The main issue was their inability to match the opponent's tempo. The character they played (The Ironclad) is referred to as the "Jock" of the game, and boasts an aggressive playstyle that heals a small amount after each combat. Healing 6 out of his 80 health may seem very minimal, but it had a strong impact on the character's game plan. This was especially evident with the card [**Bash**](https://slaythespire.wiki.gg/wiki/Slay_the_Spire_2:Bash).

**Bash** is a card that cost 2 energy, deals 8 damage, and applies the [vulnerable](https://slaythespire.wiki.gg/wiki/Slay_the_Spire_2:Vulnerable) status to the target for 2 turns. My friend saw it as a relatively bad card. playing two basic attacks would do 12 damage for the same cost, and two energy meant that they missed out on playing up to two defensive cards for at least 10 block. Additionally, there is no guarantee that they would get multiple attacks next turn, as they would prefer to block first then damage. With all this in mind, they didn't see a good time to ever use it.

I use **Bash** almost every time I see it.

The biggest upside to bash is that it allows for 50% more damage while the status is applied. This multiplier is crucial, as the best way for the Ironclad to come on top is to simply kill the opponents faster. But newer players are deterred from this. I pondered why for a while, and finally realized where the mistake was made; enemies play for tempo as well.

A lot of enemies all have a gimmick that harms stalling. Some scale strength, and some apply strong debuffs. Some even add negative status cards to your deck. If one intends to win a war of attrition, they will find themselves horribly unprepared. If the player can frontload damage, they can win faster, and therefore need to defend less. Bash may make you defend less in the moment, but the potential damage output is significantly powerful. If 6 damage is incoming, I find it worse to spend 2 energy on 10 block via (**Defend**)[https://slaythespire.wiki.gg/wiki/Slay_the_Spire_2:Defend_(Ironclad)] than to spend 1 energy on a single Defend and use the other energy to speed up the fight; even if Ironclad didn't heal after combat, you can still afford to take 1 damage 80 times.

Overall I came an important conclusion; playing for attrition is mitigating loss, not gaining an advantage. Sacrifice is important.
